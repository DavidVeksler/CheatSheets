CCW proficiency: the drills, the par times, the scorecard

Skill you cannot measure is an opinion. This page is the measurement layer: the exact courses of fire that produce a number, the published standards that number is compared against, and the diagnosis that tells you which drill to shoot next. Every drill states its distance, target, start position, string-by-string course of fire, par and scoring, because a par time quoted without those four things is not comparable to anything.

01

Cold-start card

Five numbers · 41 rounds · shoot before you warm up

Shoot these cold, in your carry gear, with the gun concealed the way you actually carry it. Cold means no warm-up string, no dry presentations in the parking lot, first shots of the day. A warm score tells you what you can do on your best day; a cold score tells you what you own.

The Wizard

2.50 spar, every string

Proves: draw to first acceptable hit on a 4″ head circle at 3, 5 and 7 yards, and a 10-yard pair, from concealment. Ken Hackathorn calls it his gold standard for whether a carrier is actually ready.

5 rounds. Pass or fail. No partial credit.

5 Yard Roundup

90 / 100instructor-level score

Proves: B-8 accuracy at speed across freestyle, strong-hand-only and weak-hand-only, all inside 2.5-second strings. Justin Dyal's benchmarks: 90 good, 95 very good, 100 clean.

10 rounds. Misses and late hits both cost points.

Bill Drill

3.00 sBill Wilson, open carry

Proves: recoil control and vision under speed, six shots to the A zone at 7 yards. Published standards genuinely disagree; see the drill entry for all three.

6 rounds. Clean or the number means nothing.

F.A.S.T.

< 7.00 sAdvanced tier

Proves: the whole manipulation chain, 2 head shots on a 3×5 card, slide-lock reload, 4 body shots on an 8″ plate. Todd Louis Green's tiers run Novice to Expert.

6 rounds. Published gear adjustments apply.

Dot Torture

50 / 50clean, at 3 yards

Proves: trigger control with no clock to hide behind. Score is the distance at which you last shot it clean, not a time.

50 rounds. Print the target; it is the instruction sheet.

The one habit that beats the whole page

Pick one named assessment, shoot it cold at the start of every range session, and write the number down. A single tracked number over a year is worth more than every drill on this page shot once and forgotten. John Daub's convention for this is simple: cold means cold, so you step to the line at your worst and you find out.

02

What the standards are calibrated against

Why 3 to 7 yards, ~3 rounds, ~3 seconds

Nearly every defensive pistol standard on this page is built for a close, short, fast problem. That shape comes from a small number of named datasets, each with real limits, and it is worth stating the limits because they bound what the numbers can honestly claim.

DatasetWhat it actually isReported figuresSelection limits, stated honestly
Tom Givens student incidentsRangemaster, Memphis; summarized by Greg Ellifritz, Jan 2022 Defensive gun uses by people who had taken Givens' training, collected over decades of teaching in one metro area. ~68 incidents. Over 90% at 3–7 yd, majority 3–5 yd. 2 at contact distance. 3 outliers at 15, 17 and 22 yd. Reported outcome: 65 wins, 0 losses, 3 forfeits (students who were unarmed when attacked). Not a random sample. These are trained, armed, motivated people in one city, and the incident set is self-reported to the instructor. It describes what happened to his students, not a national base rate.
FBI LEOKAFBI Law Enforcement Bulletin, Officer Survival Spotlight Law enforcement officers feloniously killed, compiled nationally by the FBI. Over 1985–2014, ~50.9% of officers killed with firearms were shot at 0–5 ft. Over 2005–2014, 62.2% within 0–10 ft, of which 44.8% at 0–5 ft. A different population with a different job. Officers approach people they suspect; carriers generally do not. Useful for distance, useless for round counts or for the decision to engage.
Composite incident profileJohn Daub, KR Training, from Givens, FBI and DEA sets The working profile trainers design courses of fire against. Armed robbery pattern, 1–2 assailants, 3–7 yd, very limited response time. Shorthand: three shots, three yards, three seconds. A design heuristic, not a prediction. It tells you what the standards are built for; it does not promise your problem will look like it.

Sources are linked in full in section 14. Figures are as reported by each source; none are re-derived here.

What follows from that, and what does not

Par time is scored because time is the constraint

If the problem is over in a few seconds, an untimed group tells you almost nothing about whether you can solve it. That is the entire argument for the clock.

A 25-yard bullseye score does not predict CCW competence

It measures a real and useful skill, precision under no time pressure, and that skill transfers. It just is not the skill the incident profile stresses.

Draw to first acceptable hit is the floor inside the floor

Claude Werner's argument, adopted by Daub: the shooter who lands the first solid hit seizes the initiative. Most standards on this page load heavily on that one measurement.

The distance data does not justify buying anything

It shapes what you practice and at what distance. It is not an argument for a caliber, a holster brand, or a round count in the magazine.

Outliers exist and are cheap to cover

Three of Givens' incidents were past 15 yards. Keeping a 15 and 25-yard string in your rotation costs a handful of rounds per session.

Trained-sample bias cuts both ways

A dataset of trained shooters overstates how well the average carrier does and understates how ugly an untrained response looks. Read it as a ceiling, not an average.

Shot timer basics

The beep is the only honest start signal

Anything you can anticipate, including a person saying "go," compresses your reaction time and inflates the score. Reaction to an unpredictable stimulus is part of what these drills measure.

Use a random start delay

A fixed delay teaches you to count. Dedicated timers offer a random delay (commonly a 1 to 3 second spread, sometimes fixed plus random); use it every time. The timer on this page uses a random 1.5 to 4.0 second delay.

Par mode is the reliable half of a phone app

Generating a start beep and a par beep is trivial and accurate on any device. Detecting gunshots is not.

Phone shot detection fails exactly where you need it

Indoor bay echo produces phantom shots and gunfire exceeds the microphone's dynamic range, so real shots vanish. Some apps add echo filtering; none of it is trustworthy enough to score a qualification. Use par mode on a phone, buy a dedicated timer to record splits.

Draw

Start signal to first shot. The single most-quoted number in defensive shooting, and meaningless unless the concealment garment, holster type and target size are stated with it.

Split

The interval between consecutive shots. Splits diagnose recoil control and vision; a slow first shot with fast splits is a draw problem, not a shooting problem.

Par beep

A second tone at a set interval. You are trying to fire your last shot before it sounds. This is what makes par-time drills runnable without anyone scoring you.

Post the par

Daub's exercise: when a par is easy, try to finish at exactly the par time, not under it. It builds cadence control instead of one gear.

Diagnostics: reading your own target and timer

First, throw away the correction wheel

The pie-chart "shooting correction" target on the wall of every range, the one that says a low-left group means you are heeling and a high-right group means you are thumbing, is not a diagnostic instrument for a shooter whose errors are unknown. It is widely derided by high-skill shooters and trainers, and instructors have criticized it for decades. Todd Green's write-up on pistol-training.com sets out the conditions under which it says anything at all: one-handed, slow fire, correct handedness chart, perfect sight picture. Outside those conditions it cannot tell whether there is one problem or several overlapping ones, cannot see flinching or blinking, and cannot see your grip or stance. His summary is blunt: it is a sheet of paper and it is not watching you shoot. Karl Rehn has traced the chart's origins, and the one narrow case where instructors still find it useful is double-action revolver shooters stroking the trigger.

The chart's real failure mode is that it gives a confident wrong answer. A shooter with a bad grip, a flinch and a trigger-finger placement problem shoots a low-left group, reads "heeling," works on a thing they were not doing, and gets worse. Use the tests below instead. Dry-fire safety protocol

10 rules · 6 tools · the one section on this page that is not optional

Dry practice is where most of the skill on this page gets built, and it is also where people shoot their own walls, televisions and legs. The failure mode is boringly consistent: the session ends, the gun gets loaded, and then there is one more repetition. Treat the following as a procedure, not advice.

  1. No live ammunition in the room. None.

    Not in a drawer, not in a magazine on the dresser, not in a pocket. Carry ammunition and loaded magazines go to a different room before the gun is unloaded. Physical separation creates the mental separation that keeps live and dry from mixing.

  2. Unload with a visual and a physical check, in that order, twice.

    Drop the magazine, lock the slide, look into the chamber, then put a finger in the chamber. Set the magazine down out of reach. Then do the whole check again. A revolver gets the cylinder swung out, looked at, and felt.

  3. Say it out loud.

    "This gun is unloaded and this is a dry practice session." A verbal marker is a cheap ritual that gives your brain a state change to hang the rules on.

  4. Point at a real backstop, not a convenient wall.

    An interior wall is not a backstop. A brick or concrete exterior wall, a loaded bookcase of dense books, a cast-iron bathtub, or a chest freezer will stop a common handgun round; drywall and a stud will not. Pick the direction before you start and do not change it mid-session.

  5. Nothing goes in the holster except the gun.

    A drawcord, a shirt hem, a jacket toggle or a piece of the cover garment folded into the holster mouth is the mechanism behind a large share of appendix-carry self-inflicted wounds. Look at the holster mouth before every reholster.

  6. Reholster slowly, with your eyes on the holster.

    There is no timer running on the reholster and there never will be. If you cannot see the holster, take it off the belt, holster the gun in your hand, and put it back on.

  7. End the session with a written or spoken close.

    "Session over." Then leave the room before you reload. The reload happens somewhere else, and once the gun is loaded the session is not reopened for any reason.

  8. Never reopen the session. This is the rule that kills people.

    The "one more rep after you reload" impulse is the single most common dry-practice negligent discharge. If you think of one more thing to work, write it on the list for tomorrow.

  9. Put a limit on the clock, not just on the repetitions.

    Set a timer for 15 minutes. Fatigue degrades attention before it degrades technique, and attention is the safety system.

  10. Build redundancy so that one mistake is not enough.

    Every rule above is designed to overlap another one. The goal is a system where several independent things must go wrong at once before an error becomes a discharge, because human beings make mistakes.

Common mistakes and anti-patterns

15 entries
Never drawing from concealment

Almost every published defensive standard assumes concealment, and no state qualification includes it. If your only practice is from an open range holster, your times are not the times.

Only ever shooting warm

A warm score describes your best day. Cold scores are the only measure of on-demand skill, and they are usually a full tier worse. Shoot cold first, every session.

Scoring hits but not time

Untimed accuracy is a real skill and a poor predictor of defensive competence. Without a clock you cannot tell a solid shooter from a slow one.

Scoring time but not hits

A fast miss is a miss with a receipt. Every drill here has an accuracy rule for exactly this reason; using the clock alone will make you faster at missing.

Practising exclusively at 3 yards

The incident data clusters at 3 to 7 yards, but Givens has outliers at 15, 17 and 22. A 15 and 25-yard block costs six to ten rounds a session.

The range rig that is not the carry rig

A different holster, a different belt, no cover garment, and often a bigger gun. That configuration produces numbers that belong to a person who does not exist.

Buying gear instead of buying repetitions

Every measurable variable in section 10 is worth less than a hundred dry draws a week. Fix the reps first; then see whether the gear still matters.

Treating the state qualification as a proficiency standard

A carry qualification is a legal event. Many, including the Texas LTC test, have no draw at all and use a very large target. Passing it means you are licensed, not that you are competent.

No written log, so no trend

Without a log you have a collection of anecdotes about days you shot well. The log is what turns a session into data.

Comparing your time to a video

The video was probably shot with a competition holster, no cover garment, a known start signal, and after warm-up. Compare against a published standard that states its gear assumption instead.

Never practising one-handed

Strong-hand-only and weak-hand-only strings appear in almost every serious standard here and they are where scores collapse. They are also free to train dry.

Never reloading or clearing a malfunction under a clock

Both are pure mechanics that degrade instantly under time pressure. F.A.S.T. and the 3M Test exist to expose exactly this.

Unmeasurable theatre drills

If a drill produces no number, it cannot show improvement or decline. Theatrical drills feel like training and function as entertainment.

Dry fire without a par timer

Dry practice with no clock builds a comfortable, unmeasured speed. Set a par, and reduce it in 0.5-second steps until technique breaks.

Rushing the reholster

There is no timer on the reholster, ever. This is the mistake with the worst consequence-to-benefit ratio on the list.

Colorado's process, as that page describes it, requires an in-person class from a sheriff-verified instructor with at least eight hours of instruction including a live-fire exercise and a written competency exam, with the training certificate dated within a year of application. Applicants apply through their county (or city and county) sheriff, who makes the decision; the Colorado Bureau of Investigation runs fingerprint and name-based background checks and charges $52.50 in state fees on a new application, with county administrative fees on top and varying by jurisdiction. The sheriff has 90 days after receiving the required materials, or three business days after federal background-check results arrive, whichever is later. Permits run five years, and renewals may be filed in the 120 days before expiration, typically with a refresher certificate dated within six months. Every one of those numbers is Colorado's; yours will differ.

Note what that process does and does not establish: it verifies that you attended a class and passed a competency exam. It is a legal threshold, not a proficiency ladder. That distinction is the reason this page exists.

Formal instruction is the thing this page cannot substitute for. Everything here is self-measurement, and self-measurement has a ceiling: you cannot see your own grip, your own trigger press or your own presentation. Budget at least one class a year with an instructor who will watch you shoot and tell you something you did not know.
