Selfish Reasons to Have More Kids

Bryan Caplan’s provocative case is strongest as permission to parent with less anxiety—not as proof that parenting does not matter or that every family should be bigger.

Cheat within the cheat

The 90-second verdict

The book combines empirical claims about why people differ with a value judgment about how much sacrifice good parenting requires. Keep those layers separate.

01

The central claim

Parents often overestimate how much marginal enrichment molds adult outcomes, making children feel more expensive than they need to be.

02

Best-supported part

Genes account for substantial variation in many traits, and shared-home effects on some measured adult traits are smaller than common intuition predicts.

03

Where the leap begins

Heritability does not show that a specific parenting choice is useless, that environments never matter, or that more children will improve your life.

04

The useful takeaway

Protect safety, attachment, health, and basic education. Be skeptical of exhausting optimization whose benefit is vague, tiny, or status-driven.

Quick reference

Claim → evidence → limit

“Supported” means the direction of the claim has credible evidence. It does not turn a population estimate into a prediction about your child.

ClaimEvidence statusWhat the research supportsWhat it cannot establish
Many psychological traits are heritable. Strong A 2015 meta-analysis covering 17,804 traits reported a mean twin-study heritability estimate of 49% across the included literature. Heritability is not immutability, an individual score, or the share of a trait “caused by genes.”
Shared-home influence often fades for adult cognitive differences. Strong, narrow Longitudinal twin/adoption work finds increasing genetic influence and declining shared-environment estimates for cognition with age. This pattern does not cover every outcome, every population, extreme deprivation, relationship quality, or childhood experience.
Children partly shape how parents treat them. Strong Genetically informed adoption research documents child-to-parent effects and gene–environment correlation. Bidirectional influence does not erase parent-to-child causal effects.
Parents can safely stop most enrichment. Decision-specific The evidence weakens sweeping claims that marginal tutoring, activities, or household style determines adult personality and IQ. There is no study that certifies every activity as useless. A child may value music, sport, culture, friends, or mastery now.
Low-stress parenting is better for family life. Plausible Reducing unwanted commitments can improve present family experience and parental bandwidth by definition. “Less stress” is not permission to neglect supervision, care, boundaries, treatment, or schooling.
You should have more children. Value judgment Caplan offers a long-horizon welfare argument: early costs can buy decades of valued family relationships. No genetics result can choose your desired family life, settle pregnancy risk, supply consent, or create money and support.

Fundamentals

Six terms that prevent bad conclusions

Heritability

Variation within a population

The fraction of observed differences statistically associated with genetic differences in a particular population and environment.

Example: A trait can be highly heritable while a medicine, school, or famine shifts everyone’s outcome.
Shared environment

What makes siblings alike

Family-level influences shared by siblings, such as neighborhood, household income, and broad parenting climate.

Gotcha: A low estimate does not mean the whole home is irrelevant; effects can differ by child and enter the nonshared bucket.
Nonshared environment

What makes siblings different

Different peers, teachers, illnesses, accidents, treatment, timing, and measurement error.

Example: One sibling gets a formative coach while another is injured that season.
Gene–environment correlation

Traits help select environments

A child’s genetically influenced interests and behavior can evoke responses and steer them toward particular activities.

Example: A music-obsessed child asks for lessons; the lesson–skill link is not purely parental molding.
Range restriction

Results depend on the environments studied

Findings from broadly safe, resourced homes cannot automatically describe severe neglect, toxins, malnutrition, or war.

Gotcha: “Parenting matters less within this range” is not “environment never matters.”
Causal effect

A change produced by an intervention

Correlation between parenting and outcomes can reflect genes, reverse causation, selection, or a true parental effect.

Example: To isolate causation, researchers use adoption, twins, natural experiments, or randomized interventions—not resemblance alone.

The book’s argument

Four claims, steelmanned and bounded

1

Mold less; enjoy the relationship more.

If an extra hour of parent-chosen optimization has little durable effect, its opportunity cost matters. The strongest version of Caplan’s advice is to stop buying low-confidence future gains with high-confidence present misery.

Concrete useQuit Tuesday travel practice when the child dislikes it and the whole household loses dinner and sleep.
BoundaryKeep the activity when the child loves it, it anchors friendships, or stopping would break a meaningful commitment.
2

Count the full life, not only the toddler bottleneck.

Family-size decisions are made during the years when children require the most hands-on labor. Caplan asks parents to include possible adult relationships, holidays, mutual aid, and grandchildren in the ledger.

Concrete useImagine an ordinary Tuesday at ages 35, 50, 65, and 80—not just a holiday photo—and ask which household you want at each stage.
BoundaryFuture closeness is not guaranteed. Children are people, not retirement assets, caregivers, or a grandchild delivery system.
3

Price children using your actual parenting plan.

If your estimate assumes a larger house, elite activities, constant chauffeuring, and zero paid help, you may be costing a cultural script rather than another child.

Concrete useCompare “one more child with shared rooms, public school, and a weekly sitter” against your real budget—not an imagined premium package.
BoundaryChildcare, housing, health care, leave, and lost earnings remain real. Optimism is not a financing plan.
4

More people can create value, but this is philosophy and economics.

Caplan argues that another person can enjoy a life, form relationships, work, create, and solve problems. This is a case for human potential—not a conclusion delivered by twin studies.

Concrete useInclude the future child’s possible welfare and contribution in the moral ledger, not only parental inconvenience.
BoundaryReasonable people disagree about population ethics, environmental externalities, and duties to possible people.

Reasons to have a big family—and reasons not to

Make a joint life decision, not a debate-club victory

A good reason is specific to the life you and your partner want. It survives contact with consent, health, money, support, and the needs of children already here.

Reasons that can support “yes”

01

You want another person, not another baby.

The desire includes the difficult ages and the adult’s independence—not only pregnancy, infancy, or a family image.

02

You like the daily texture of family life.

Noise, shared meals, sibling negotiation, repeated routines, and a busy home feel meaningful more often than oppressive.

03

Your horizon is longer than the next three years.

You willingly accept a front-loaded season of work for the chance of several lifelong parent–child relationships.

04

Your systems have reusable capacity.

Gear, routines, rooms, skills, school knowledge, and caregiver relationships make the next child less like starting from zero.

05

You have support you can actually name.

A partner, relatives, friends, paid care, flexible work, or community can cover illness, appointments, and parental recovery.

06

Both partners can give an unpressured yes.

The decision respects the person bearing medical risk and the person sharing decades of care.

Reasons to pause or stop

01

Either partner’s answer is no.

A child is a two-yes decision. Persuasion can clarify facts; it cannot manufacture consent.

02

Health risk is unacceptable.

Pregnancy, postpartum health, infertility treatment, disability, or genetic risk may dominate abstract benefits. Use individualized medical advice.

03

The current family is already underwater.

Untreated depression, violence, addiction, chronic sleep collapse, or a relationship near failure needs stabilization—not a motivational slogan.

04

The budget only works in the best case.

If one job loss, childcare change, or health event causes insolvency, build margin before adding a dependent.

05

Existing children have unmet high needs.

Another child may be possible, but the real plan must preserve treatment, supervision, and secure attention already required.

06

The desire belongs to an audience.

Religion, parents, social media, ideology, or fear of regret can inform you; none will do the 3 a.m. caregiving.

Private worksheet

Readiness gates

These are conversation prompts, not a score that can make the decision for you.

0 of 8 gates discussed

Working knowledge

Lower the cost without lowering the floor

The floor is safety, warmth, food, sleep, health care, appropriate supervision, boundaries, and education. Optimize everything above it only when the family gets enough value back.

Subtract

Cancel low-value commitments

Audit activities by child enjoyment, durable skill, relationships, cost, travel, and sleep.

Example: Keep swimming for water safety; drop a second league nobody enjoys. Gotcha: Do not quit the child’s passion merely because driving is inconvenient.
Buy time

Spend on the bottleneck

A cleaner, sitter, meal kit, carpool, or nearby housing may buy more family welfare than premium toys.

Example: A four-hour sitter twice a month protects the marriage. Gotcha: Recurring help belongs in the real budget.
Systemize

Replace heroics with defaults

Use repeatable meals, shared calendars, hand-me-down bins, automatic refills, and age-appropriate chores.

Example: Five rotating weeknight dinners remove 260 annual decisions. Gotcha: A system that only one parent maintains is hidden labor.
Relationship

Invest where memory lives

Warmth, repair, attention, humor, and respect matter even when they do not permanently alter IQ or personality.

Example: Apologize after yelling and redo the conversation. Gotcha: “Genes matter” is never an excuse for contempt.
Boundaries

Use discipline for present family life

Clear rules and predictable consequences make the home workable now; they need not promise a redesigned adult personality.

Example: Devices charge outside bedrooms at a fixed time. Gotcha: Consequences should be proportionate, known, and nonviolent.
Media

Judge quality, context, and displacement

The 2026 AAP framework moves beyond one universal “screen-time” number toward content, design, co-use, and what media replaces.

Example: A family film is not equivalent to an addictive feed at midnight. Gotcha: A convenient break can still crowd out sleep, movement, or connection.

Common mistakes

What the argument does not license

“Heritable” means “unchangeable.”
No. Heritability describes variation in a studied population. Eyeglass need is heritable; glasses still change vision. A uniform intervention can change everyone while leaving rank-order variation highly heritable.
“Small shared-environment effect” means “parents do not matter.”
No. Parents shape safety, attachment, culture, memories, access, treatment, present happiness, and which environments children encounter. Some effects may be child-specific rather than shared by siblings.
“Average effects are small” predicts my child.
No. Population averages hide heterogeneity. A particular teacher, diagnosis, move, peer group, or intervention can matter enormously to one child even when an average effect is modest.
“Relax” means ignore medical, developmental, or educational problems.
No. A low-stress philosophy still responds to hearing loss, dyslexia, depression, abuse, unsafe behavior, and other actionable conditions. Do not turn skepticism about marginal optimization into neglect.
More children guarantee happier parents or close siblings.
No. The book offers a wager about a whole life. Temperament, health, resources, relationships, and chance affect the result. Adult children owe neither closeness nor caregiving.
A family-size spreadsheet can settle a two-person commitment.
No. A worksheet exposes assumptions and missing plans. It cannot override either partner’s no, quantify pregnancy risk, or convert uncertainty into consent.

Follow the evidence trail

This guide distinguishes Caplan’s thesis from later empirical work. Links go to the publisher or original research.

Book

Selfish Reasons to Have More Kids

Basic Books’ official page for Bryan Caplan’s thesis, edition details, description, and publication information.

Twin meta-analysis

Polderman et al. (2015), Nature Genetics

Meta-analysis of 17,804 traits from 2,748 publications; the broad empirical anchor for substantial genetic influence across human differences.

Development

Briley & Tucker-Drob (2013), Psychological Science

Longitudinal twin/adoption meta-analysis finding increasing heritability and declining shared-environment estimates for cognition across development.

Adult IQ

McGue et al. (2021), Psychological Science

Follow-up of adoptive and biological families at mean offspring age 32, estimating genetic and environmentally mediated contributions to adult IQ variation.

Parenting

Reiss et al. (2023), SRCD Monographs

Adoption-study synthesis showing genetic, parent-to-child, and child-to-parent processes working together in family development.

Media

AAP (2026), Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents

Current policy statement emphasizing media content, design, context, and displacement rather than treating every screen exposure as equivalent.
