Carryover cooking

Rise after heat is removed · 8 combinations

Carryover scales with two things: how much mass is holding heat, and how steep the temperature gradient was going in. A thin cut loses heat to the air almost as fast as the surface stops cooking; a thick roast keeps driving heat inward for many minutes after it leaves the oven.

Why ground meat is different

Grinding distributes any surface bacteria through the entire volume. A whole steak's interior was never exposed, so searing the outside handles the risk; ground meat has no unexposed interior, so the whole mass must reach 160°F/71°C.

Why whole-muscle rare is treated as safe

The 3-minute rest at 145°F/63°C is not a rounding convenience: FSIS states temperature holds or keeps rising during that window, which is what actually delivers the pathogen reduction, the same principle used in inspected processing plants.

Low & slow: the stall isn't a plateau to fight

Brisket & pork shoulder
Why the stall happens

As surface moisture evaporates, it cools the meat at roughly the same rate the pit is adding heat. Internal temperature holds nearly flat, often for hours, typically somewhere in the 150–170°F/66–77°C band.

What wrapping changes

Wrapping in foil or butcher paper ("the Texas crutch") traps surface moisture so it stops evaporating, removing the cooling effect and pushing the temperature climbing again. It does not add heat itself.

Why "done" is a feel, not a number

Collagen converts to gelatin over time at high sustained temperature; brisket and pork shoulder are judged probe-tender, meaning the probe should slide in with almost no resistance, commonly around 200–204°F/93–96°C, but the texture, not the number, is the actual endpoint.

Ribs use bend, not probe-tender

The same collagen logic applies, but the practical test is the bend/crack test on the rack rather than a probe, since rib meat is too thin to probe reliably.

Poultry specifics

Breast vs. thigh, whole-bird probing
Why breast and thigh diverge

Thigh meat is darker, fattier and more connective-tissue-rich; it stays moist and tender well past 165°F, so many cooks pull it to 175–185°F for texture. Breast dries out quickly above its 165°F minimum.

Pop-up timers overcook

They are fixed to breast-adjacent fat and calibrated conservatively; by the time they pop, the breast is typically well past 165°F.

The pink-near-the-bone myth

Pink meat next to bone in young birds is usually residual myoglobin/marrow pigment leaching into the bone-adjacent tissue during fast cooking, not a sign of undercooking, provided the measured internal temperature met the 165°F/74°C minimum.

Brining changes the carryover math

A brined bird holds more water, which changes how fast it comes up to temperature and how much it carries over. Probe by temperature, not by the clock you used for an unbrined bird.

Ice-point calibration

Pack a glass with crushed ice, top with water, stir, insert the probe without touching the glass: it should read 32°F/0°C. Adjust or replace if it's off by more than a couple of degrees.

Boiling-point calibration

At sea level, boiling water reads 212°F/100°C. Boiling point drops with elevation, roughly 1°F for every 500 ft of elevation gain, so account for altitude before assuming the thermometer is wrong.

Probe placement rule

Insert into the thickest part, away from bone and fat pockets, without letting the tip touch the pan or grate. Bone conducts heat differently and a touched surface reads hotter than the food.

No brand recommendations here

Accuracy claims vary by model and lot; verify any specific thermometer against the ice-water check above rather than trusting a spec sheet.

Holding, danger zone & reheating

40–140°F / 4–60°C
2-hour rule

Perishable food should not sit in the danger zone more than 2 hours total; cut that to 1 hour if the ambient temperature is above 90°F/32°C.

Hot holding

Keep hot food at 140°F/60°C or above continuously: "keep hot foods hot," not just cooked once and left to drift.

Reheating

Leftovers and casseroles must reach 165°F/74°C when reheated, regardless of the temperature they were originally cooked to.

Sous vide holding looks like a violation and isn't

Holding food at, say, 140°F/60°C for hours is inside the danger-zone range by the numbers, but it is a controlled pasteurization hold at a validated time-temperature pair (see Sous vide). The rule is written for unmonitored, uncontrolled holding, not a calibrated water bath running a known lethality table.

Source: USDA FSIS, "Danger Zone (40°F–140°F)" and "How Temperatures Affect Food": https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f. The FDA Food Code applies a related but distinct 4-hour time/temperature-control provision to retail and food-service operations; the 2-hour figure above is FSIS's consumer-facing rule.

Common mistakes

7 stop conditions
Cooking to final instead of pull temperature

Ignoring carryover means a turkey breast or thick roast overshoots by up to 10°F past the intended final temperature.

Probing through to the pan or bone

A grate- or bone-touching tip reads hotter than the meat and produces a false "done" reading.

Trusting the oven's own probe without calibration

Run the ice-water check first: a built-in probe can be off by several degrees out of the box.

Resting a thin steak until it's cold

Thin cuts carry over almost nothing and lose their sear-crisp exterior the longer they sit; rest briefly, not by a fixed long timer borrowed from a roast.

Applying the whole-muscle rare rule to ground meat

A rare burger has no unexposed interior: the 160°F/71°C ground-meat minimum has no safe lower option, unlike a steak.

Using the pre-2011 pork number

160°F pork is safe but dry and outdated; 145°F/63°C plus a 3-minute rest has been the FSIS standard since May 2011.

Treating a sous vide minimum time as a maximum

The Baldwin/FSIS tables give a floor for pasteurization, not a ceiling. Pulling at the minimum time on a whim, rather than after it, skips the safety margin entirely.
