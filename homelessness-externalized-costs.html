Plate I

The ledger

Who pays when the invoice stops at city hall?

Emergency care, jail and shelter appear in public accounts. Lost access, damaged property, extra security and harm to people living outside often do not. A budget-only comparison therefore leaves out relevant costs. That does not tell us their total, or establish which response produces the lowest full cost.

Plate II

Seen and unseen

Two columns, four quadrants, and only one pairing ever gets argued about.

A ledger with one column is not a ledger. Set the costs of homelessness against the costs of preventing it, then split each column into what lands in accounts and what does not. Four quadrants appear, and public argument almost always runs between the top two.

The asymmetry that decides most arguments. Budget-based debate compares the top-left quadrant against the top-right one. That is incomplete on both sides, and it is incomplete unevenly: Column A’s unseen costs are diffuse and unowned, while Column B’s unseen costs are concentrated and politically owned. A cost with an owner gets defended in a hearing. A cost spread across everyone who no longer sits down downtown gets nothing, because nobody is assigned to notice it. Diffusion is not evidence of smallness; it is evidence of who shows up.

The causes are unseen too. Column B is not only a purchase decision. Preventing homelessness partly means undoing rules that were each adopted for a stated reason — safety, neighborhood character, worker protection — and whose costs fall on people who were never at the hearing. Plate XII takes those up, along with what the evidence does and does not support about them.

The seen

A repair invoice is visible. The purchase forgone to pay it is not. Bastiat’s seen and unseen asks what resources could have produced in the alternative use. A large budget is neither proof of benefit nor proof of waste. Bastiat, What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen

A different broken window

Wilson and Kelling’s broken windows concerns disorder, informal control and policing. It makes empirical claims about behavior. Bastiat’s opportunity-cost logic does not validate a particular policing tactic. Wilson & Kelling, Broken Windows; OJP abstract

The unseen side of each decision
DecisionWhat the accounts showWhat the ledger must also ask
Spend on a programAppropriation, contract and service output.What alternative use of the same resources was displaced, and what additional outcome did this spending buy?
Do not build capacityA hospital, service or low-cost housing expense avoided.Do crises shift to emergency departments, jail, families or streets? Count substitution, not just the budget saving.

The common-property lens: when occupying a park or sidewalk prevents others from using it, access has been transferred away from those users. That is an economic loss of shared use, not a legal finding of a compensable taking. The same ledger includes the shelter and safety needs of the people occupying it.

Plate V

Public-space exclusion

Loss of access can matter without appearing as a purchase.

A sidewalk detour, an abandoned park visit or a trip avoided after harassment has a cost. A public space need not close officially to become costly to use. This could be a major omission, but the evidence does not establish it as the largest category.

Plate VI

The hardening of public space

A removed bench leaves no invoice. It leaves an absence.

Every category so far counts something that somebody bought. Adaptation mostly consists of things somebody stopped buying. Taking out a bench, locking a restroom, capping a fountain, shortening park hours or declining to replace a drinking fountain all enter public accounts as reduced expenditure. The loss falls on everyone who used that amenity and appears in no line item.

The sign is inverted here. In every other plate, a larger cost shows up as a larger number. In this one, the cost shows up as a smaller number. A ledger assembled from budget documents will therefore read the removal of a commons as fiscal prudence. That is an accounting artifact, not a finding about welfare.

Three mechanisms, and why each hides differently
MechanismWhat happensWhy it escapes the ledger
RemovalSeating, restrooms, fountains, shelter, outlets and open hours are withdrawn.Recorded as a saving. There is usually no dated inventory of what existed before, so the counterfactual amenity level is unrecoverable.
HardeningThe amenity is provided, but specified to resist sleeping, sitting, storage or concealment.The defensive features are priced inside the unit, not billed separately. No vendor publishes the surcharge for a louvered door or an anti-graffiti panel.
SubstitutionThe function migrates to private property, conditioned on a purchase.The cost lands on non-customers, and moves without any transaction at all when a corporate policy changes.

It does not establish a total, and it cannot: hostile design also targets skateboarding, vandalism, crowd control and vehicle attack, and amenity removal is frequently driven by maintenance budgets, liability and water-quality rules that have nothing to do with anyone sleeping outside. The honest statement is that this is a large omission of unknown size, and that the reason it is unknown is that no one was ever required to count.

Plate VII

Avoidance and the commute

The cheapest way not to use a place is to live somewhere else.

The strongest form of an unseen cost is not a missing bench. It is the household that resolves the problem by moving out, keeps its job in the center, and then pays for that resolution twice a day for twenty years in a currency that enters no city's accounts. Plate V priced what proximity does to a house. This plate concerns the people who left, and what leaving costs them afterward.

Read this against Plate III, carefully. The default example lands near $9,000 a year, the same order of magnitude as Denver's measured program cost of $12,078–$15,484 per participant-year. The symmetry the ledger requires. The same arithmetic prices the commute of a hospital cleaner or a line cook pushed out of the same city by its housing costs, who drives the same hours in the opposite direction. Both are avoidance costs; only one of them is usually described that way. A ledger that counts the first and not the second is telling half the story, and the same worksheet answers both.

Hospital savings, displaced care

Closing inpatient capacity can shift care to families, emergency departments, jails or the street when community alternatives are inadequate. Better treatments and the failures of old institutions also matter. The capacity decline does not by itself quantify how much homelessness it caused or identify an appropriate bed target. Treatment Advocacy Center, state-hospital census, 2023 data

The missing bottom rung

Single-room occupancy and boarding-house restrictions can remove low-cost rooms from the feasible housing mix. HUD describes SROs and the regulatory constraints on recreating them. Counting neighborhood improvement while ignoring displaced room supply is another incomplete ledger. HUD, Considering SRO Housing, June 2018

gap The counterfactual: no dollar total here measures the net benefit of beds or rooms that were never built. Such a calculation needs service quality, operating costs, rights and safety effects, and the alternatives people actually used. “Spend less” alone does not answer it.

Supply and entry restrictions

The same accounting problem runs one step further back. If the bottom rung of the housing market and the bottom rung of the labor market were removed by rule rather than by markets, then part of the cost in Plate I is a cost of those rules. The evidence here is uneven: one link is strong, one is priced but advocacy-sourced, one is a single credible study in a contested literature, and the total is not decomposable at all.

The political column

Column B’s unseen half is not measured in dollars at all. Reopening involuntary psychiatric capacity means litigating civil commitment. Enforcing camping bans means enforcement against people with nowhere to go. Repealing zoning, code and wage rules means telling identifiable constituencies that the protection they were promised is being withdrawn. None of these has a price tag, and all of them have a cost.

What this plate is not doing. Every rule named above was adopted for a stated reason, and several of them deliver real benefits: lead paint rules, fire codes and habitability standards prevented deaths that no one now has to count. The claim here is not that the rules are wrong. It is narrower and harder to dispute: their costs land on people who were not at the hearing, in a column nobody totals, and a comparison that omits that column is not an argument about costs at all.

Plate XIV

What the numbers don’t say

An incomplete ledger warrants better measurement, not invented precision.

Common accounting mistakes
Tempting claimWhat the evidence actually permitsWhat would close the gap
“The full cost is a multiple of the budget.”External costs are omitted, but the multiplier and national total are not established.Same-place, same-year costs with attribution and overlap removed.
“Public space is the largest cost.”Plausible as a hypothesis; not ranked by the evidence here.Comparable welfare estimates across categories, including people outside property markets.
“Nearby crime was committed by homeless people.”Proximity measures place, not identity or causal effect.Offense-level data, exposure and credible comparison conditions.
“A capital price is an annual cost.”A building asset lasts beyond the budget year; operations recur.Consistent annualization, useful life and financing assumptions.
“Add property losses, security and lost visits.”Market prices can already incorporate anticipated safety and access losses.Choose a valuation approach or explicitly reconcile overlaps.
“Vacancies prove business flight caused by encampments.”Observed vacancy is affected by several simultaneous changes.Matched locations, pre-trends, encampment timing and competing explanations.
“If a category is unmeasured, its cost is zero.”Missing evidence is not a zero, but also not permission to assume a positive number of any size.A disclosed sensitivity range and a measurement plan, separate from empirical findings.
“Removing the bench saved money.”The budget falls and the cost moves to everyone who used it. Removals enter accounts with the opposite sign from their welfare effect.A dated amenity inventory, per-installation stated rationale, and a valuation of forgone use.
“Living far out is a preference, so the commute is not a cost.”Preference and avoidance look identical in a commute time. The sorting literature identifies the avoidance channel for crime, not its size for homelessness.Matched movers with stated and revealed reasons, and origin conditions measured before the move.
“Hostile architecture is a homelessness cost.”Defensive design also targets skateboarding, vandalism, crowding, loitering and vehicle attack; amenity removal is often driven by maintenance and liability.Per-installation rationale and dates, matched against exposure in comparable places.
“Deregulation would have prevented it, so the rules caused it.”Rent levels predict where homelessness concentrates, and land-use rules raise land prices. Neither result identifies how many episodes a specific rule caused.Policy variation with credible timing, and outcomes measured on inflow rather than on point-in-time counts.
“Prevention costs are just the program budget.”The prevention column also contains political and rights costs — commitment standards, enforcement against people with nowhere to go, withdrawn protections — that no appropriation shows.State them explicitly and unpriced rather than dropping them, the same way a gap is disclosed.
“One less expense means one more dollar of social benefit.”Taxes and transfers differ from resources consumed; fixed budgets differ from marginal costs.Report fiscal impact separately from net resource and welfare effects.

The question the ledger makes possible: compared with a specific alternative, what resources are consumed and what harms remain, for whom, over what period? Housing, treatment, public order and non-intervention all belong inside that question. No policy prescription is implied by a missing cell.

How to read the sources and evidence tags

Measured means a recorded count, reported expenditure, budget or market observation. Budgets and reported cases are not causal estimates. Estimated means a modeled association, survey estimate, unit-cost calculation or explicitly hypothetical arithmetic. Gap means this review has no defensible applicable value, not that nobody has ever studied the subject.

Each numeric entry links to its source and names its period or cohort. The nearby tag also covers its numeric units and dates. Source titles and plate numbers are navigation, not claims of measurement. Historical snapshots remain historical; dollar figures have not been silently inflated to current prices.

The sources include original studies, agency and operator reports, and explicitly labeled reporting where a primary budget was not verified. Evidence of a category’s existence is separate from its size, causation and preventability.
