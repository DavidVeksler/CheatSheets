Homelessness / a free-market account of the hidden social cost

Remove the bottom rungs.
The street becomes the fallback.

Restrict the room someone can afford, the work they can obtain, and the care available when they cannot live independently. The need survives. The cost moves to the street, the family, the emergency room—and everyone sharing the city.

Three ways to remove the bottom rung

“Marginal” means the option just within someone’s reach—not a judgment about the person.

01 / Housing

A room can be inadequate by regulation and indispensable to its tenant.

Minimum standards & use restrictions→Fewer low-cost rooms→Higher entry price for shelter

The mechanism. A person may prefer a small room with shared facilities to a larger apartment they cannot afford. Rules that prohibit that product remove a feasible bargain between landlord and tenant. The relevant comparison is the room versus the person’s actual fallback.

The economic loss. The forgone benefit of an affordable room belongs in the ledger alongside construction and compliance costs. Higher rent is a burden on the tenant; it is not automatically an equal net social loss, because part can be a transfer to a property owner. Lost housing supply, displaced consumption and preventable exposure require separate accounting.

The 2018 Furman Center model summarized by HUD compares 126 studios at $1,820 monthly rent with 251 rooms at $1,040, sharing kitchens and bathrooms, in the same 67,100-square-foot building envelope. ($1,820 − $1,040) × 12 = $9,360; 251 − 126 = 125 additional units if the smaller format is built.

What the calculation means: nearly twice the units and a roughly 43% lower rent in that model. It prices the accommodation choice that restrictions can foreclose. It is not a current rent quote, an estimate of a pure regulatory tax, or a count of people who would otherwise be homeless. Smaller rooms provide fewer amenities.

02 / Work

A higher legal wage does not guarantee a job at that wage.

Wage floors & entry barriers→Some bargains no longer viable→Income and skill-building forgone

The mechanism. If an employer’s expected value from a particular hire falls below the required cost, that job may not be offered. Licensing and other entry costs can also obstruct self-employment. The free-market objection is that a willing worker can lose an attainable foothold when the law demands a better one.

The empirical test. Employers can also absorb costs, raise prices or adjust profits; wage floors can raise income for people who remain employed. The net effect depends on the setting. Seth Hill’s published study estimates a 2–3% relative increase in homeless counts following a 10% year-over-year real minimum-wage increase, using 203 localities in 2006–2019. It finds evidence consistent with employment disruption; it does not identify every person made homeless by a lost job.

The economic loss. Count forgone output, income insecurity and lost opportunities to acquire skills. A transfer payment is not itself destroyed output, and the worker’s lost wages cannot simply be added to the full value of the same lost production.

The free-market response: permit more entry-level work and lower barriers to earning independently. Judge the outcome for people who lose access as well as for those receiving higher wages. Evidence for the broad claim that all marginal work has been outlawed is not established here.

03 / Psychiatric care

Closing an institution does not eliminate the need for care.

Legal, financing & capacity constraints→Unmet need for sustained care→Families, emergency rooms, jails and streets

The mechanism. For some people with severe mental illness, a room and a job are insufficient. If necessary care is unavailable and community support cannot substitute, the burden can move to relatives and crisis systems. That is a displaced cost, not proof of a saving.

Commitment is restricted

O’Connor v. Donaldson (1975) bars confining a nondangerous person who can live safely outside an institution, alone or with willing help, merely on the basis of mental illness. It did not outlaw psychiatric hospitals or all involuntary treatment. State commitment criteria matter.

Financing is restricted

The federal Medicaid IMD exclusion generally restricts federal payment for adults aged 21–64 in psychiatric institutions with more than 16 beds. CMS’s Section 1115 program allows participating states to seek exceptions for eligible short-term care. A financing restriction is distinct from a ban on operating a hospital.

Capacity is a separate constraint

The 2023 state-hospital census reports 36,150 beds, or 10.8 per 100,000 people. This advocacy organization’s census measures one part of the system, not all care available and not how many people need commitment.

The economic loss. Count unmet treatment need, suffering, family caregiving and repeated crisis response. Compare these with treatment resources, institutional harms and the loss of liberty under compulsory care. Lower spending by one agency can coexist with a larger burden elsewhere.

The policy test: make appropriate residential treatment feasible and finance care according to need rather than administrative location. Compulsory treatment also requires an explicit rights argument, due process and review. It is not the same kind of voluntary exchange as renting a room or taking a job.

Reopening options has costs too

The three mechanisms can reinforce each other: a more expensive room requires more income, employment disruption makes that room harder to retain, and untreated illness can make either arrangement fail. Their effects cannot be added as if they describe separate people.

Changing housing and work rules confronts constituencies that benefit from the restrictions. Changing commitment policy raises direct questions of liberty and clinical judgment. The case for reform must count those costs and the costs imposed on people who were absent from the original hearing.

The economic consequence

Society pays twice: for the option lost and the crisis that replaces it.

A larger emergency budget is only the visible end of the chain.

01 / The opportunity destroyed

The room, the work, the stable life that never happens.

The tenant loses a feasible home; the worker loses an attainable exchange and its path to experience; a person needing care loses the prospect of greater stability. These losses can occur before a public agency spends anything.

Economic category: gains from exchange and well-being forgone. The relevant alternative must actually be feasible.

02 / The resources diverted

Other people must spend time and resources coping.

Families provide crisis care. Businesses buy security. Public services respond repeatedly. Residents reroute, move or lose access to shared space. Those people and resources could otherwise have done something else.

Economic category: additional labor, capital and time consumed relative to the alternative.

03 / The harm left over

Even after the spending, people still bear losses.

A cleanup invoice does not measure exposure, victimization, interrupted treatment, fear or lost freedom to use a neighborhood. People living outside bear central parts of this burden, alongside others affected by it.

Economic category: remaining harm and lost use that the spending did not remedy.

The cost attributable to a restriction

Compare these losses with the restriction and under a feasible reform, including the reform’s costs and any safety, income or rights benefits the restriction preserves.

“Pays twice” describes lost opportunity followed by coping costs; it is not permission to count the same loss twice. Rent paid to an owner is not all destroyed wealth. Lost wages and lost output overlap. Property values may already reflect safety and amenity losses. The accounting limits apply to every number.

The free-market question: how much of this burden could have been avoided by allowing the room and the job, and making appropriate care available? That requires tracing each policy’s effect; the entire cost of homelessness cannot be assigned to the three mechanisms simply because they exist.

The political mechanism proposed here. Budget-based debate compares the top-left quadrant against the top-right one. That is incomplete on both sides, and it is incomplete unevenly: Column A’s unseen costs are diffuse and unowned, while Column B’s unseen costs are concentrated and politically owned. A cost with an owner gets defended in a hearing. A cost spread across everyone who no longer sits down downtown gets nothing, because nobody is assigned to notice it. This is an explanation of how costs can receive unequal attention, not a measurement of their relative size.

The causes are unseen too. Column B is not only a purchase decision. Preventing homelessness partly means undoing rules that were each adopted for a stated reason — safety, neighborhood character, worker protection — and whose costs fall on people who were never at the hearing. Three ways to remove the bottom rung takes those up, along with what the evidence does and does not support about them.

The seen

A repair invoice is visible. The purchase forgone to pay it is not. Bastiat’s seen and unseen asks what resources could have produced in the alternative use. A large budget is neither proof of benefit nor proof of waste. Bastiat, What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen

A different broken window

Wilson and Kelling’s broken windows concerns disorder, informal control and policing. It makes empirical claims about behavior. Bastiat’s opportunity-cost logic does not validate a particular policing tactic. Wilson & Kelling, Broken Windows; OJP abstract

The common-property lens: when occupying a park or sidewalk prevents others from using it, access has been transferred away from those users. That is an economic loss of shared use, not a legal finding of a compensable taking. The same ledger includes the shelter and safety needs of the people occupying it.

The policy implication

Restore the options. Then measure the result.

Managing the downstream burden and removing an upstream restriction answer different questions.

Housing / restore choice

Let the inexpensive room be offered.

Change to evaluate: allow smaller dwellings, individually leased rooms and shared facilities where restrictions prevent them.

Look for: additional low-cost rooms, lower entry rents and fewer people losing housing—not just approval of more expensive construction.

Count against the gain: construction resources, any additional safety harms and effects on other users of the property.

Mechanism and evidence ↑
Work / restore entry

Let more people get a foothold.

Change to evaluate: reduce barriers to entry and examine whether wage requirements exclude workers at the edge of employability.

Look for: hiring, hours, total earnings and housing retention among affected workers, including people who otherwise would not get hired.

Count against the gain: lower pay for retained workers, unsafe arrangements and other costs—not just the number of jobs.

Mechanism and evidence ↑
Care / restore access

Make sustained treatment a feasible alternative.

Change to evaluate: remove avoidable financing and capacity barriers; examine commitment rules separately from voluntary treatment.

Look for: access to appropriate care, continuity, improved functioning and fewer repeated crises.

Count against the gain: treatment costs, institutional harm, coercion and lost liberty. A free-market case for voluntary exchange does not by itself justify confinement.

Mechanism and evidence ↑

The cheaper alternative may be the one we prohibited. Judge a restriction against the housing, work or care someone could actually obtain without it—and judge reform against the full cost it leaves behind.

The hardening of public space
Evidence / The hardening of public space

The hardening of public space

A removed bench leaves no invoice. It leaves an absence.

Adaptation can consist of things a city stops providing: a bench, a restroom, a drinking fountain or open park hours. A reduction in operating costs can appear in the budget; the loss to former users does not appear as a corresponding expense. Whether removal saves money overall and why it happened must be established for the particular case.

The sign is inverted here. In every other plate, a larger cost shows up as a larger number. In this one, the cost shows up as a smaller number. A ledger assembled from budget documents will therefore read the removal of a commons as fiscal prudence. That is an accounting artifact, not a finding about welfare.

Three mechanisms, and why each hides differently
MechanismWhat happensWhy it escapes the ledger
RemovalSeating, restrooms, fountains, shelter, outlets and open hours are withdrawn.Recorded as a saving. There is usually no dated inventory of what existed before, so the counterfactual amenity level is unrecoverable.
HardeningThe amenity is provided, but specified to resist sleeping, sitting, storage or concealment.The defensive features are priced inside the unit, not billed separately. No vendor publishes the surcharge for a louvered door or an anti-graffiti panel.
SubstitutionThe function migrates to private property, conditioned on a purchase.The cost lands on non-customers, and moves without any transaction at all when a corporate policy changes.

Seating, hours, audio deterrents, power outletsgap No inventory, no cost series, no removal dates.Closing this gap needs a dated amenity census, per-installation stated rationale, and exposure matched against comparable places and periods. Until that exists, the single largest adaptation category has no number at all.

What this plate can and cannot claim. It establishes that the category is real, that it is systematically excluded by budget-based accounting, and that a handful of instances have been priced. It does not establish a total, and it cannot: hostile design also targets skateboarding, vandalism, crowd control and vehicle attack, and amenity removal is frequently driven by maintenance budgets, liability and water-quality rules that have nothing to do with anyone sleeping outside. The honest statement is that this is an omission of unknown size; the examples here do not establish a national total or a single explanation for the measurement gap.

Avoidance and the commute

The cheapest way not to use a place is to live somewhere else.

The strongest form of an unseen cost is not a missing bench. It is the household that resolves the problem by moving out, keeps its job in the center, and then pays for that resolution twice a day for twenty years in a currency that enters no city's accounts. the “Public-space exclusion” section examines what proximity does to a house. This plate concerns the people who left, and what leaving costs them afterward.

Sensitivity worksheet / not an empirical estimate

What would an omitted cost change?

estimated All starting values below are hypothetical assumptions chosen for this worked example. The range is not a confidence interval, a published city estimate or a bound inferred from the case studies above. Source: the explicit arithmetic in this worksheet.

For a defined cohort and year, let B be usual-service public cost, E the net external cost per person-year, and A the alternative’s full social cost on the same basis. Then street total = B + E; the alternative’s arithmetic advantage is B + E − A. A negative result favors the baseline under those assumptions.

estimated Hypothetical street total: $35,000–$50,000 per person-year.
Alternative advantage: −$5,000 to $10,000 per person-year. Break-even external cost: $10,000 per person-year.

Include in A: recurring services, annualized capital, remaining emergency use, external harms and burdens of intervention. Include in E only incremental harms not already counted in B. For total rather than incremental comparisons, include the same outcome categories on both sides. No real-world preference follows until inputs and outcomes are defensible.

What the numbers don’t say
Evidence / What the numbers don’t say

What the numbers don’t say

An incomplete ledger warrants better measurement, not invented precision.

Common accounting mistakes
Tempting claimWhat the evidence actually permitsWhat would close the gap
“The full cost is a multiple of the budget.”External costs are omitted, but the multiplier and national total are not established.Same-place, same-year costs with attribution and overlap removed.
“Public space is the largest cost.”Plausible as a hypothesis; not ranked by the evidence here.Comparable welfare estimates across categories, including people outside property markets.
“Nearby crime was committed by homeless people.”Proximity measures place, not identity or causal effect.Offense-level data, exposure and credible comparison conditions.
“A capital price is an annual cost.”A building asset lasts beyond the budget year; operations recur.Consistent annualization, useful life and financing assumptions.
“Add property losses, security and lost visits.”Market prices can already incorporate anticipated safety and access losses.Choose a valuation approach or explicitly reconcile overlaps.
“Vacancies prove business flight caused by encampments.”Observed vacancy is affected by several simultaneous changes.Matched locations, pre-trends, encampment timing and competing explanations.
“If a category is unmeasured, its cost is zero.”Missing evidence is not a zero, but also not permission to assume a positive number of any size.A disclosed sensitivity range and a measurement plan, separate from empirical findings.
“Removing the bench saved money.”The budget falls and the cost moves to everyone who used it. Removals enter accounts with the opposite sign from their welfare effect.A dated amenity inventory, per-installation stated rationale, and a valuation of forgone use.
“Living far out is a preference, so the commute is not a cost.”Preference and avoidance look identical in a commute time. The sorting literature identifies the avoidance channel for crime, not its size for homelessness.Matched movers with stated and revealed reasons, and origin conditions measured before the move.
“Hostile architecture is a homelessness cost.”Defensive design also targets skateboarding, vandalism, crowding, loitering and vehicle attack; amenity removal is often driven by maintenance and liability.Per-installation rationale and dates, matched against exposure in comparable places.
“Deregulation would have prevented it, so the rules caused it.”Rent levels predict where homelessness concentrates, and land-use rules raise land prices. Neither result identifies how many episodes a specific rule caused.Policy variation with credible timing, and outcomes measured on inflow rather than on point-in-time counts.
“Prevention costs are just the program budget.”The prevention column also contains political and rights costs — commitment standards, enforcement against people with nowhere to go, withdrawn protections — that no appropriation shows.State them explicitly and unpriced rather than dropping them, the same way a gap is disclosed.
“One less expense means one more dollar of social benefit.”Taxes and transfers differ from resources consumed; fixed budgets differ from marginal costs.Report fiscal impact separately from net resource and welfare effects.

The question this argument leaves with the reader: before paying to manage people on the street, which affordable rooms, attainable jobs and necessary treatment options have we made unavailable—and what would restoring them change? Count the lost opportunities and the burdens shifted to others, then compare reforms on outcomes and rights as well as budgets.

How to read the sources and evidence tags

Measured means a recorded count, reported expenditure, budget or market observation. Budgets and reported cases are not causal estimates. Estimated means a modeled association, survey estimate, unit-cost calculation or explicitly hypothetical arithmetic. Gap means this review has no defensible applicable value, not that nobody has ever studied the subject.

Each numeric entry links to its source and names its period or cohort. The nearby tag also covers its numeric units and dates. Source titles and section labels are navigation, not claims of measurement. Historical snapshots remain historical; dollar figures have not been silently inflated to current prices.

The sources include original studies, agency and operator reports, and explicitly labeled reporting where a primary budget was not verified. Evidence of a category’s existence is separate from its size, causation and preventability.
